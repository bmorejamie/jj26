Building this site with agents

When I sit down to design a site, the first thing I do is fire up Figma. For this one, that was the last thing I wanted to do.

It was time to see what a design-to-code workflow is really all about: design the site while agents built it, at the same time.

At first I worried I wasn't actually designing my website. After a few iterations I realized I was, but it was different. My instinct was to reach for Figma, but I resisted. We kept iterating. The agents built and suggested things, and after a while I realized I wasn't thinking about Figma anymore.

Instead of starting with a pixel-perfect design, I started with a general direction I knew I wanted and kept steering toward it. There were detours here and there, but I ended up where I was headed, with some pleasant surprises along the way.

I found it really freeing to work directly in code. I could do things that would be really hard to visualize or prototype in Figma, like my little Workin’ Day & Night animation. I spent more time thinking about the content of the site and the overall UX. And I got to play with more interesting interactive things, like the 3D device scroll animations in the Work section, or subtle touches like the animated logo.

I learned new ways to work, too. I discovered the magic of tuning panels: an instant, custom control panel that pops up right on the element you want to fine-tune. They became integral to my workflow, and they can be anything you want. Amazing. Try them out.

Some ideas I don't think I'd have reached in Figma at all, like a little pixel-art Michael Jackson moonwalking when you hit the day/night toggle.

Day and night mode itself was something I'd always thought of as mostly a gimmick, a designer-portfolio thing. Designing this way changed my mind. I use dark mode most of the time, a lot of people have their devices set to it, and on a phone in a dark room it's just better. It's real UX, and I appreciate it a lot more now.

The logo animation could have eaten several evenings in After Effects, and then it would still need to be coded. Instead I worked in the tab next to my prompt window, iterating with Claude until we got it (thank you, tuning panels), and we kept thinking of things to add. Now it's an ever-evolving little logo mark, and it sparked the wordmark animation too.

There were times I got frustrated and thought I could do it in Figma in five minutes, exactly like I wanted. But it turns out exactly what I wanted wouldn't have been as good as how it ended up. And it certainly wouldn't have been as fun. Or educational. And it really got the gears turning…
