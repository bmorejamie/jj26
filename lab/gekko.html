The trading signal I never used

I was paying for a trading indicator every month and barely using it.

Not because it's bad. It's great. But using it means sitting in TradingView, opening a tab for every stock, switching timeframes, waiting on it. So my mornings went like this: listen to a crypto show while getting ready, sit down to "just check," poke around for half an hour, act on nothing. My workout was the first thing to go.

I don't have time for all that. That's the whole point of this project.

The team

Gekko is a dashboard for my own money, built by the same little team of agents from the last couple of entries. Each one has a lane.

Live — Gekko team jjux · Oct 2026

Pick one. They hand work to each other through a shared vault, not through me.

The expensive model does the thinking. The cheap ones do the reading.

Designing it from the couch

Most of the design happened on my iPad, on the couch, on a Sunday morning after a late night. Claude would put up a version, I'd poke at it, and we'd go again. Plenty didn't land. I tapped a dial that was supposed to do something and nothing happened. The background was too blue. Ditch the serif. Real feedback, real fast.

UX first, always. The pretty stuff can come later.

Mock data lies

My favorite moment so far was a pair trade view: hold one of two stocks, and switch when one runs way ahead of the other. The first version ran on made-up numbers, and the made-up numbers said it wasn't worth the trouble. I half-joked that maybe we should drop the whole idea.

Then Claude read the real signals straight off my own chart, and the real numbers said the opposite. Mocks are for layout. Get to real data as fast as you can.

Same night, I didn't fully understand what I was looking at, and I'm the one it's for. I'm just a simple human meat brain. That's a design bug, not a me bug. The next version was built on what I already read every day, the chart and the indicator's buy and sell arrows, with the answer right on top: hold this one, nothing to do.

The rules

It's my money, so the guardrails came before the features.

Interactive — guardrails rule → why

  • They watch, alert and explain. I make every trade myself. Maybe someday a small test account, but only after paper trading wins for weeks.
  • Balances and holdings live on my machine only. The shared vault gets ideas and decisions, never figures. This page gets none of either.
  • Every key the agents hold is read-only. They can see data. They can't move anything.
  • TopBot built a clone so we could scan more stocks at once. It doesn't count until it matches the real thing on my real charts.
  • The agents review each other's code. I review the designs. Nobody grades their own homework.

Speed is easy. Trust is the product.

The human catches the bug

One night it hit me: TopBot runs in one endless session. It never starts fresh, so it never gets that moment where it catches up on its memory, the way Claude does. That's why it kept making things up. None of the agents caught that. Now the vault has an index, and they check it before they claim something.

Even our crude little homebrew setup is working.

What's next

The real goal is my mornings. The top of the dashboard should be anything I need to act on today, a setup that's starting to form, or "nothing today, go work out." Once I trust it, I stop flipping through tabs. And I get my workout back.
